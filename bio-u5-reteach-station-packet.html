HS-LS4-1: Communicate scientific information that common ancestry and biological evolution are supported by multiple lines of empirical evidence.

HS-LS4-2: Construct an explanation based on evidence that evolution results primarily from four factors: the potential for a species to increase in number, heritable genetic variation, competition for limited resources, and the proliferation of organisms better able to survive and reproduce.

Teacher Guide

There are 8 stations. Groups of 3 or 4 students rotate through them. Each station has a direction page and a few questions on the Station Recording Sheet. Students carry this one sheet from station to station. Most stations take about 15 to 20 minutes, so all 8 stations take about 2 to 3 class periods (in a 90-minute period, about 4 stations). You can also pick only the stations that match what your students missed on the exit tickets.

Some stations need no prep beyond printing the direction page. Others need cut-out cards in envelopes. Every station ends with a Reset step so the next group finds it ready.

Required materials

  • Colored pencils (5 colors per student, Station 1)
  • 2 envelopes: label them 3 and 7
  • Hole punch and tan, green, and red paper (about 40 dots of each), a small cup, and a timer (Station 5)
  • Scissors
  • Color printing for Stations 2, 5, and 6 if possible; laminator for the Habitat Mat (optional)

Station 1 setup: Homologous Limbs

A coloring activity: students color the same bone groups in a human arm, cat leg, bat wing, and whale flipper.

  1. Print one Limb Coloring page per student (the page is marked as a station material).
  2. Set out colored pencils (at least 5 colors) and the direction page.
Tip: Ask: "If an engineer designed a wing and a flipper from scratch, would they use the same parts?"

Station 2 setup: Vestigial Structures

"Guess, then flip" picture cards: wisdom teeth, goosebumps, kiwi wings, cave fish eyes, ear muscles, and the third-eyelid fold.

  1. Print the 3 Station 2 flip-card pages. Cut each page down the middle to make 6 cards and fold each on the dashed line.
  2. Set out the cards and the direction page.
Tip: Students can test two of these on themselves: try wiggling your ears, and look in a mirror for the pink fold in the corner of your eye.

Station 3 setup: Homologous or Analogous?

A card sort: 8 pairs of structures sorted into homologous (shared ancestry) or analogous (same job, separate origins).

  1. Print the Station 3 cut-out page. Cut out the 2 heading cards and 8 pair cards. Put them in Envelope 3.
  2. Set out the envelope and the direction page.
Tip: The octopus and human eye pair surprises students: both are camera-like eyes, but they develop differently.

A data station with real (approximate) cytochrome c differences: students rank organisms by relatedness to humans.

  1. Set out the direction page.

Station 5 setup: Camouflage Hunt

A hands-on natural selection model: students "hunt" colored paper dots on a printed leaf-litter habitat for three generations.

  1. Print the Habitat Mat in color (laminate if you can).
  2. Use a hole punch to make about 40 dots of each color from tan, green, and red paper (tan should match the mat). Put them in a cup.
  3. Set out a timer, the mat, the cup, and the direction page.
Tip: This matches the Peppered Moth lab. Ask students to connect the tan dots to the moths on matching bark.

A data station based on the Grants' real finch study: students compare beak-depth graphs before and after a drought.

  1. Set out the direction page.
Tip: Point out that no bird grew a deeper beak. Deep-beaked birds survived and passed the trait to their chicks.

Station 1: Homologous Limbs

Homologous structures have the same basic bones in different organisms, even when they are used for very different jobs. They are evidence that these organisms share a common ancestor. Every forelimb below has the same bone groups in the same order, from the shoulder down:

1 upper arm bone (humerus) → 2 forearm bones (radius and ulna) → wrist bones (carpals) → hand bones (metacarpals) → finger bones (phalanges)

Directions:

  1. Take a copy of the Limb Coloring page. Pick 5 colored pencils and fill in the color key.
  2. Color each bone group the same color on all four limbs.
  3. Compare the limbs and answer the Station 1 questions.

Station 2: Vestigial Structures

A vestigial structure is a body part that has lost most or all of its original job. It was useful to an organism's ancestors. Because it does little harm, it has not completely disappeared. Vestigial structures are evidence that species change over time and share ancestors with organisms that still use those parts.

Directions:

  1. Look at the 6 picture cards with your team. For each card, talk it over: what part do you think is vestigial, and what was its original job?
  2. Write your team's prediction on your recording sheet.
  3. When your team has predicted all 6, flip the cards open. Write the actual answer.
  4. Reset: fold every card closed.

fold back on the dashed line
Wisdom teeth

Our ancestors had bigger jaws and chewed tough raw plants. Today many people's jaws are too small, so wisdom teeth get stuck (shown tilted at the back) and are often removed.

2. Skin on a cold arm: What is the vestigial structure?
fold back on the dashed line
Goosebumps (tiny hair muscles)

Tiny muscles pull each hair up. In furry mammals this fluffs the fur to trap heat or look bigger. Humans have so little body hair that it does almost nothing.

Station 3: Homologous or Analogous?

Homologous structures have the same basic structure because they came from a common ancestor, even if their jobs are different (a human arm and a whale flipper).

Analogous structures do the same job but are built in completely different ways. They evolved separately because the organisms faced similar challenges. They are not evidence of a close common ancestor.

Both are wings for flying. But a butterfly wing is a thin sheet of chitin with veins and no bones. A bird wing has bones, muscles, and feathers. These are analogous.

Directions:

  1. Open Envelope 3. Lay out the two heading cards.
  2. Sort the 8 pair cards under Homologous or Analogous. Ask: do they share the same inside structure, or only the same job?
  3. Record your sort and answer the questions. Reset: put all cards back in Envelope 3.

Station 4: Protein Evidence

All living things that use oxygen make a protein called cytochrome c. It is a chain of about 104 amino acids. Because the DNA instructions for it are inherited, closely related species have very similar cytochrome c. The more differences, the longer ago the two species shared a common ancestor.

The table shows about how many amino acids in each organism's cytochrome c are different from the human version (from published protein comparisons).

  1. Rank the organisms from most closely related to humans to least closely related. Record the ranking.
  2. Use the data to answer the Station 4 questions.
Molecular evidence (comparing DNA and proteins) lets scientists compare organisms that look nothing alike, like a human and a yeast.

Station 5: Camouflage Hunt

Natural selection happens when individuals with certain traits survive and reproduce more than others. Over generations, helpful traits become more common. In this model, paper dots are prey and you are the predator.

Directions: (one predator, one timer, one recorder; switch jobs each round)

  1. Spread 10 dots of each color on the Habitat Mat. Record the starting numbers.
  2. The predator looks away, then turns around and picks up dots one at a time for 15 seconds.
  3. Count the dots left on the mat (the survivors) by color.
  4. Survivors reproduce: add one new dot of the same color for each survivor.
  5. Repeat for 3 generations. Then answer the questions.
  6. Reset: put all dots back in the cup.
The predator must pick up the first dot they see. No searching for a certain color!

Station 6: Darwin's Finches in a Drought

On the Galápagos island Daphne Major, scientists Peter and Rosemary Grant measured hundreds of medium ground finches every year. In 1977 a drought killed most small, soft seeds. Only big, hard seeds were left. Finches with deeper beaks could crack them.

Modeled data that follows the real pattern the Grants measured: the average beak depth rose by about half a millimeter.

Directions: Study the two graphs and answer the Station 6 questions.

Station 7: Three Kinds of Selection

Natural selection can push a trait in different directions:

  • Directional selection: one extreme is favored, so the whole curve shifts that way.
  • Stabilizing selection: the average is favored, so the curve gets narrower and taller.
  • Disruptive selection: both extremes are favored, so the curve splits into two peaks.

Directions:

  1. Match each graph to a type of selection. Record it.
  2. Open Envelope 7. Sort the 8 real-world cards under the matching graph.
  3. Record your sort. Reset: put all cards back in Envelope 7.

Station 8: How New Species Form

A species is a group of organisms that can mate and have fertile offspring. A new species can form when two populations stop mating with each other. This is called reproductive isolation. Over many generations, the two groups change in different ways until they become separate species.

Type of isolationWhat keeps the groups apart
Geographica physical barrier: a canyon, river, mountain, or ocean
Behavioraldifferent courtship: songs, dances, colors, or signals
Temporaldifferent timing: they breed at different times of day or year

Directions:

  1. Read each of the 6 cards. Decide which type of isolation it shows. Write your prediction.
  2. Flip each card open to check.
  3. Reset: fold every card closed.

Answer Key

Station 1: Homologous Limbs

1. Bat: the finger bones (phalanges) and hand bones are very long to hold up the wing. Whale: the bones are short and flat, with extra finger bones, all inside the flipper.

2. Human: grasping and lifting; cat: walking and running; bat: flying; whale: swimming and steering.

3. They inherited the same bone plan from a common ancestor. Over time natural selection changed the sizes and shapes for different jobs.

Station 2: Vestigial Structures

1. 1: Wisdom teeth; 2: Goosebumps (tiny hair muscles); 3: Tiny wings; 4: Eyes covered by skin; 5: Ear muscles; 6: The pink fold in the corner.

2. A body part that no longer has its original job (or has a much smaller job), left over from ancestors that used it.

3. Their ancestors could see. They likely came from river fish with working eyes, and the eyes were lost after many generations in the dark.

Station 3: Homologous or Analogous?

1. Homologous: Human arm and Whale flipper; Cat front leg and Bat wing; Frog front leg and Human arm; Horse front leg and Seal flipper. Analogous: Butterfly wing and Bird wing; Shark body shape and Dolphin body shape; Octopus eye and Human eye; Hummingbird beak and Butterfly mouth tube.

2. Both are shaped for fast swimming, but the shark is a fish with a cartilage skeleton and gills, and the dolphin is a mammal with bones and lungs. Their body shapes evolved separately.

3. Homologous structures, because the same inner structure was inherited from a shared ancestor. Analogous structures only show similar environments or jobs.

Station 4: Protein Evidence

1. Chimpanzee (0), Rhesus monkey (1), Dog (11), Horse (12), Chicken (13), Rattlesnake (14), Tuna (fish) (21), Baker's yeast (fungus) (44).

2. A chicken: 13 differences vs. 21 for tuna, so humans share a more recent common ancestor with chickens.

3. Dogs and horses are about equally related to humans (all are mammals that share a common ancestor at about the same time).

4. Every organism has DNA and proteins, so they can be compared even when there are no bones or similar body parts.
