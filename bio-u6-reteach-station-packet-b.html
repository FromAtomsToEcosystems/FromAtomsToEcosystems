Teacher Guide

There are 8 stations. Groups of 3 or 4 students rotate through them. Each station has a direction page and a few questions on the Station Recording Sheet. Students carry this one sheet from station to station. Most stations take about 15 to 20 minutes, so all 8 stations take about 2 to 3 class periods (in a 90-minute period, about 4 stations). You can also pick only the stations that match what your students missed on the exit tickets.

Some stations need no prep beyond printing the direction page. Others need cut-out cards in envelopes. Every station ends with a Reset step so the next group finds it ready.

Required materials

  • 5 envelopes: label them 1, 2, 4, 6 (and a spare)
  • A paper bag with 150 to 250 white beans and a cup of about 30 red beans (Station 8)
  • Scissors; calculators (Stations 7 and 8)

Station 3: Wolves Return to Yellowstone

Gray wolves were removed from Yellowstone National Park by the 1920s. In 1995 and 1996, 31 wolves were brought back.

YearWolves in YellowstoneElk in the northern herd
19940about 17,000
1995 to 199631 releasedabout 16,000
2003about 170about 9,000
2012about 80about 4,000

Rounded from National Park Service counts. Hunting, drought, and bears also affected the elk.

After wolves returned, scientists saw young aspen and willow trees growing taller along some streams, where elk had been eating them. Beavers, which use willows, became more common.

Station 5: Crowded or Not?

Some limiting factors get stronger when a population is crowded (density-dependent). Others hit the same no matter how crowded it is (density-independent). Predict which.

Directions:

  1. Look at the 6 cards at this station. Do not open them yet.
  2. For each card, talk it over with your team and write your prediction.
  3. When you have predicted all of them, flip the cards open to check. Write the actual answer.
  4. Reset: fold every card closed.

Station 8: Bag of Beans: Mark and Recapture

Ecologists cannot count every fish in a lake. They capture some, mark them, let them go, and later capture another sample. The fraction of marked animals in the second sample lets them estimate the whole population.

Population ≈ (number marked × number in 2nd sample) ÷ marked in 2nd sample

Directions: The white beans in the bag are a fish population.

  1. Without looking, scoop out about 20 beans. Count them. Replace each one with a red bean (marked fish). Put the red beans in the bag and set the white ones aside.
  2. Shake the bag well. Scoop out a second sample. Count all the beans and the red beans in it.
  3. Use the formula to estimate the population. Then count every bean in the bag to check.
  4. Reset: put all the white beans back in the bag and the red beans back in the cup.

Answer Key

Station 1: Ecology Word Match

1. Population: One species in one area (All the sea otters in Monterey Bay); Community: All the species in one area (Otters, urchins, kelp, and fish together); Ecosystem: Living things plus nonliving things (The kelp forest, water, and sunlight); Habitat: Where an organism lives (its address) (Rocky reef in cold water); Niche: An organism's role (its job) (Otter eats urchins and keeps kelp safe); Limiting factor: Something that keeps a population from growing (Not enough urchins to eat); Carrying capacity: The largest population an area can support (About 3,000 otters on the California coast (modeled)); Keystone species: Has a much bigger effect than its numbers (Sea otter).

2. Habitat is where it lives (address); niche is what it does there (job).

Station 2: Levels of Organization

1. Organism: One red-tailed hawk, A single oak tree; Population: All the coyotes in a canyon, A school of 500 anchovies; Community: Every plant, animal, and fungus in a meadow, All the organisms living in a pond; Ecosystem: A pond: its organisms, water, mud, and sunlight, A desert with its plants, animals, sand, and heat; Biosphere: All life on Earth and where it lives, Every ecosystem on the planet together.

2. A community is living things only; an ecosystem adds the nonliving parts.

Station 3: Wolves Return to Yellowstone

1. Elk fell from about 16,000 to about 4,000 by 2012.

2. Willow → elk → wolf.

3. Wolves eat or scare elk, so elk eat fewer young willows; willows grow, and beavers have more food and building material. This is a trophic cascade.

Station 4: A Carbon Atom's Trip

1. B E C A F D (A carbon atom is part of CO₂ in the air; A grass blade takes it in by photosynthesis; The carbon becomes part of glucose in the grass; A deer eats the grass; The deer dies, and decomposers break down its body; Decomposers release the carbon as CO₂ by cellular respiration).

2. Out: photosynthesis. Into: cellular respiration by decomposers.

3. No; atoms are never used up, they cycle.

Station 5: Crowded or Not?

1. 1: Density-dependent; 2: Density-independent; 3: Density-dependent; 4: Density-independent; 5: Density-dependent; 6: Density-independent.

2. Answers vary. Dependent: competition for nests, parasites. Independent: hurricane, volcano, pollution spill.

Station 6: Crack the Relationship Code

1. +/+ Mutualism: Fungi on plant roots trade nutrients for sugar, Ants protect an acacia tree that feeds them; +/0 Commensalism: Barnacles ride on a gray whale, A spider builds a web on a fence plant; +/− Parasitism: A tapeworm in a dog's gut, Mistletoe growing into an oak branch; +/− Predation: A bobcat catches a rabbit, An orca eats a seal; −/− Competition: Lions and hyenas want the same zebra, Two weeds compete for the same sunny spot.

2. A predator kills and eats its prey quickly. A parasite lives on or in its host and harms it over time.

Station 7: Ocean Energy Pyramid

1. Krill 50,000; small fish 5,000; tuna 500; sharks 50 kcal.

2. Small fish may increase (fewer predators); sharks may decrease (less food).

3. Only about 10% of energy passes up each level, so little energy is left at the top.

Station 8: Bag of Beans: Mark and Recapture

1. Data varies. Example: 20 marked; second sample 25 with 3 marked; estimate = 20 × 25 ÷ 3 ≈ 167.

2. Take bigger samples or repeat and average.

3. Marked individuals must mix evenly with the rest, like real animals moving around.
