Teacher Guide

There are 8 stations. Groups of 3 or 4 students rotate through them. Each station has a direction page and a few questions on the Station Recording Sheet. Students carry this one sheet from station to station. Most stations take about 15 to 20 minutes, so all 8 stations take about 2 to 3 class periods (90-minute periods: about 4 stations per day). You can also pick only the stations that match what your students missed on the exit tickets.

Some stations need no prep beyond printing the direction page. Others need a few cut-out cards in envelopes. Every station ends with a Reset step so the next group finds it ready.

Required materials

  • 6 to 8 large paper clips (Station 1)
  • 9 envelopes: label them 1, 4A, 4B, 4C, 5, 7A, 7B, 7C (and one spare)
  • Scissors; glue and construction paper (optional, Station 2)
  • A timer or clock with a seconds hand (Station 6)
  • Color printing for Stations 3, 7, and 8 if possible; card stock for Station 7 (optional)
  • Laminator (optional: makes cards last for years)

Station 1 setup: Monomers and Polymers

Students model dehydration synthesis and hydrolysis with a paper clip chain and water cards, then match polymer cards to their monomers.

  1. Print the Station 1 cut-out page. Cut out the monomer, polymer, and water cards and put them in an envelope labeled Envelope 1.
  2. Set out 6 to 8 paper clips (large ones are easiest to hook).
  3. Set out one copy of the Station 1 direction page.
Tip: Ask students: "How many links are in a chain of 6 clips?" before they count the water cards. Most groups say 6 at first.

Station 2 setup: Food Detectives

Like the "guess, then flip" cards in other station packets: students predict which macromolecule each food is richest in, then fold open the card to check.

  1. Print the 4 Station 2 flip-card pages (2 cards per page). Cut each page down the middle to make 8 tall strips.
  2. Fold each strip on the dashed line so the answer hides behind the picture. (Optional: glue onto construction paper first so it lasts longer.)
  3. Set out the 8 cards face up with the direction page.
Tip: If you have real food labels or empty packages, set them out too. Students love comparing the grams of fat, carbohydrate, and protein.

Station 3 setup: Indicator Test Results

A data station (no chemicals needed). Students read pictures of indicator results for 6 mystery samples and decide which macromolecules each one has.

  1. Set out the Station 3 direction page and the Test Results page (print in color if you can; the colors matter).
  2. Optional: if you ran the food test lab, set out real tubes from that day for students to compare.
Tip: Remind students that Benedict's has a color scale. Green and yellow still mean some simple sugar.

Station 4 setup: Build a Feedback Loop

Students build three negative feedback loops (too hot, too cold, blood sugar after a meal) by placing cards on a loop mat.

  1. Print the Feedback Loop Mat (laminate it if you can) and the Stations 4 and 5 cut-out page.
  2. Cut out the Station 4 cards. Put each scenario row (6 cards) into its own envelope: 4A, 4B, and 4C.
  3. Set out the mat, the 3 envelopes, and the direction page.
Tip: Students mix up the sensor and the control center. Ask: "Which part only notices? Which part decides?"

Station 5 setup: Negative or Positive Feedback?

Students compare two graphs and sort 8 real examples into negative and positive feedback.

  1. Cut out the Station 5 cards from the Stations 4 and 5 cut-out page (2 heading cards and 8 example cards). Put them in Envelope 5.
  2. Set out the direction page with the two graphs.
Tip: Fruit ripening is a positive feedback loop in plants: ripe fruit gives off ethylene gas, which makes nearby fruit ripen and give off more ethylene.

Station 6 setup: Pulse Check

A hands-on data station: students measure their pulse at rest, after 1 minute of exercise, and during recovery, then graph it.

  1. Set out a clock or timer with a seconds hand (a phone timer works).
  2. Clear a small space for jumping jacks, or use a sturdy step.
  3. Set out the direction page.
Tip: Students who cannot exercise can still do the station as timer and recorder and graph a teammate's data.
Tip: This pairs with the Exercise and Homeostasis lab if you want to compare to class data.

Station 7 setup: Lock and Key

A cut-and-fit puzzle: students match substrates to enzyme active sites, see that heated (denatured) enzymes no longer fit, and see that the enzyme is unchanged after the reaction.

  1. Print the Station 7 cut-out page in color on card stock if you can. Cut out each piece on the dashed lines (cut the notches, too).
  2. Put the enzymes A, B, C and substrates 1 to 5 in Envelope 7A. Put the three heated enzymes in Envelope 7B. Put the three split substrates in Envelope 7C.
  3. Set out the 3 envelopes and the direction page.
Tip: Cutting the notches cleanly matters. A craft knife or extra time with scissors makes the pieces fit nicely.
Tip: If you laminate the pieces, they will last for many years of classes.

Station 8 setup: Reading Enzyme Graphs

A graph-reading station: students find optimum temperature and pH, explain denaturing, and explain why the rate levels off with more substrate.

  1. Set out the Station 8 direction page and the Enzyme Graphs page (color helps but is not required: the lines are solid and dashed).
Tip: Graph 3 has no units on purpose. Ask students to describe the shape first ("rises fast, then flattens"), then explain it.

After the stations

Collect the recording sheets and check them with the answer key, or have groups trade and check. A quick follow-up: give the matching Day 2 exit ticket or warm-up from the unit to see who still needs help.

Station 1: Monomers and Polymers

Living things build very large molecules called macromolecules. Most of them are polymers: long chains made of small building blocks called monomers. Cells join monomers by dehydration synthesis. Each new link removes one water molecule. Cells break polymers apart by hydrolysis. Each broken link uses one water molecule.

Directions:

  1. Each paper clip is one monomer. Build a chain of 6 paper clips. Every time you make a link, set one water card to the side.
  2. Count your water cards. Record the number on your recording sheet.
  3. Now break your chain into single clips. Give back one water card for every link you break.
  4. Open Envelope 1. Lay the 4 monomer cards in a row. Place each polymer card under the monomer it is built from.
  5. Answer the Station 1 questions on your recording sheet.
  6. Reset: unhook the clips and put every card back in Envelope 1.

Station 2: Food Detectives

Every food is made of the same four kinds of macromolecules: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. Most foods have more than one kind. But most foods are richest in one kind (after water). Carbohydrates give quick energy, lipids store a lot of energy, and proteins build and repair the body.

Directions:

  1. Look at the 8 food cards at this station. Do not open them yet.
  2. With your team, decide which macromolecule each food is richest in. Write your prediction on your recording sheet.
  3. When your team has a prediction for all 8, flip each card open to check. Write the actual answer next to your prediction.
  4. Answer the other Station 2 questions.
  5. Reset: fold every card closed so the next group cannot see the answers.

Station 4: Build a Feedback Loop

Homeostasis means keeping conditions inside the body stable, close to a set point (like 37 °C for body temperature). The body does this with feedback loops. Every loop has the same five parts:

  • Stimulus: a change away from the set point.
  • Sensor: detects the change.
  • Control center: compares the change to the set point and sends a signal (often the brain).
  • Effector: the muscle, gland, or organ that acts.
  • Response: what happens, which moves conditions back toward the set point.

Directions:

  1. Open Envelope 4A. Read the scenario card. Place the other 5 cards in the correct boxes on the Feedback Loop Mat.
  2. Check your loop with your team: does each step cause the next one?
  3. Write the effector and response on your recording sheet. Then do the same for Envelopes 4B and 4C.
  4. Reset: put each set of cards back in its own envelope.

Station 5: Negative or Positive Feedback?

Most feedback loops in the body are negative feedback: the response reverses the change, so conditions return to the set point. A few are positive feedback: the response makes the change bigger and bigger until an event is finished. Then the loop stops.

Directions:

  1. Study Graph A and Graph B with your team. Decide which graph shows negative feedback and which shows positive feedback.
  2. Open Envelope 5. Place the two heading cards side by side. Sort the 8 example cards under the correct heading.
  3. Record your sort and answer the Station 5 questions.
  4. Reset: put all cards back in Envelope 5.

Station 6: Pulse Check

When you exercise, your muscles use oxygen faster and make more carbon dioxide and heat. Your body responds right away: your heart beats faster and you breathe harder. When you stop, these responses slow down again. This is homeostasis you can measure!

How to take a pulse: press two fingers (not your thumb) on the side of your neck or the thumb side of your wrist. Count beats for 15 seconds, then multiply by 4 to get beats per minute.

Directions: (one person exercises, one counts time, one records)

  1. Sit quietly for 1 minute. Take your resting pulse. Record it at minute 0.
  2. Do jumping jacks (or step up and down on the bottom stair step) for 1 minute.
  3. Take your pulse right away, and again after 1, 2, 3, and 4 minutes of sitting. Record each one.
  4. Graph your data on your recording sheet. Then answer the questions.
Safety: If you have a health reason not to exercise, be the timer and recorder, and use a teammate's data.

Station 7: Lock and Key

Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions. The molecule an enzyme acts on is its substrate. The substrate fits into a pocket on the enzyme called the active site, like a key fits a lock. Each enzyme fits only certain substrates. After the reaction, the enzyme is not used up; it can be used again. High heat or the wrong pH can change an enzyme's shape. This is called denaturing.

Directions:

  1. Open Envelope 7A. Try to fit each substrate into each enzyme. Record which substrate fits each enzyme.
  2. Open Envelope 7B. These are the same enzymes after being heated to 70 °C. Try the substrates again. Record what happens.
  3. Open Envelope 7C. These show what the substrates look like after the reaction. Compare them to the enzymes. Record what changed and what did not.
  4. Reset: put every piece back in its own envelope.

Station 8: Reading Enzyme Graphs

Every enzyme has an optimum: the temperature and pH where it works fastest. Too far from the optimum, the enzyme slows down. If it gets too hot, the enzyme denatures and stops working. The amount of substrate matters too: more substrate means more collisions, until every active site is busy.

Directions:

  1. Look at the three graphs on the Enzyme Graphs page at this station.
  2. Use the clues below and the graphs to answer the Station 8 questions on your recording sheet.
Clues: Human body temperature is about 37 °C. Some bacteria live in hot springs near 75 °C. The stomach is very acidic (pH about 2). The small intestine is slightly basic (pH about 8).
