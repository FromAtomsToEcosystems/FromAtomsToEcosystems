Teacher Guide

There are 8 stations. Groups of 3 or 4 students rotate through them. Each station has a direction page and a few questions on the Station Recording Sheet. Students carry this one sheet from station to station. Most stations take about 15 to 20 minutes, so all 8 stations take about 2 to 3 class periods (in a 90-minute period, about 4 stations). You can also pick only the stations that match what your students missed on the exit tickets.

Some stations need no prep beyond printing the direction page. Others need cut-out cards in envelopes. Every station ends with a Reset step so the next group finds it ready.

Required materials

  • 2 envelopes: label them 2 and 7
  • Dry-erase markers or yarn and tape; a laminator for the Food Web Mat (Station 2)
  • 1 or 2 dice (Station 8)
  • Scissors
  • Color printing for Stations 1, 4, and 8

Station 1 setup: Abiotic or Biotic?

A picture-reading station: students classify 12 numbered items in an ecosystem scene as biotic or abiotic.

  1. Set out the direction page (print in color).
  2. Optional: add a tray of real items (a rock, a leaf, a cup of soil, a pinecone) for students to classify too.
Tip: The dead log and mushrooms are the tricky ones: once-living things and decomposers are biotic.

Station 2 setup: Build a Food Web

Students place 12 organism cards on a trophic-level mat and draw the arrows of a food web.

  1. Print the Food Web Mat (laminate it so students can draw with dry-erase markers) and the Station 2 cards. Cut out the 12 cards and put them in Envelope 2.
  2. Set out the mat, envelope, dry-erase markers (or yarn and tape), and the direction page.
Tip: The most common mistake is backwards arrows. Say: "The arrow points to the mouth."
Tip: The owl pellet lab pairs well with this station.

Station 3 setup: The 10% Rule

A calculation station: students apply the 10% rule to a four-level energy pyramid and explain where the energy goes.

  1. Set out the direction page and calculators if you like.
Tip: Some students multiply by 10 instead of dividing. Ask: "Should there be more or less energy at the top?"

Station 1: Abiotic or Biotic?

An ecosystem includes all the living things in an area and the nonliving things around them.

  • Biotic factors are living or once-living parts of an ecosystem: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria, dead leaves, and fallen logs.
  • Abiotic factors are nonliving parts: sunlight, water, air, temperature, soil minerals, and rocks.

Directions: Look at each numbered item in the picture. On your recording sheet, name it and decide if it is biotic (B) or abiotic (A). Then answer the questions.

Station 2: Build a Food Web

A food chain shows one path of energy: who eats whom. A food web shows many connected food chains. Arrows point from the food to the eater, in the direction energy flows.

  • Producers make their own food from sunlight (plants).
  • Primary consumers eat producers. Secondary consumers eat primary consumers. Top consumers are eaten by no one.
  • Decomposers (fungi and bacteria) break down dead things and return nutrients to the soil.

Directions:

  1. Open Envelope 2. Read each organism card. Place it in the correct band on the Food Web Mat.
  2. Use a dry-erase marker (or yarn) to connect every food to its eaters with arrows.
  3. Answer the Station 2 questions. Reset: erase the mat and put the cards back.

Station 3: The 10% Rule

An energy pyramid shows how much energy is at each trophic level. Only about 10% of the energy at one level is passed to the next level. The other 90% is used for life (moving, growing, staying warm) and is lost as heat from cellular respiration, or is never eaten.

How to calculate: energy at the next level = energy at this level × 0.10 (move the decimal one place left).

Directions: Fill in the missing energy for each level on your recording sheet. Then answer the questions.

Example: 5,000 kcal of plants → about 500 kcal in the animals that eat them.

Station 4: Who Benefits?

Organisms in a community interact in different ways. We can describe each relationship by who is helped (+), harmed (-), or not affected (0).

RelationshipEffectMeaning
Mutualism+ / +both benefit
Commensalism+ / 0one benefits, the other is not affected
Parasitism+ / -the parasite benefits; the host is harmed (usually not killed quickly)
Predation+ / -the predator kills and eats the prey
Competition- / -both need the same limited resource

Directions:

  1. Look at the 8 picture cards. Do not open them yet.
  2. For each card, predict the relationship and the symbols (+, -, 0).
  3. Flip each card open to check. Reset: fold every card closed.

Station 5: Population Growth Curves

When resources are unlimited, a population grows faster and faster: exponential growth (a J-shaped curve). In real ecosystems, limiting factors like food, water, space, and disease slow growth. The population levels off at the carrying capacity: the largest population the environment can support (an S-shaped, logistic curve).

Part 1: A student grew yeast in a flask of sugar water and counted cells (thousands per mL) every 2 hours. Graph this data on your recording sheet.

Modeled class data.

Part 2 (real story): In 1944, 29 reindeer were released on St. Matthew Island, Alaska, which had no predators. By 1957 there were about 1,350; by 1963, about 6,000. They ate the slow-growing lichen faster than it could grow back. After a hard winter, in 1966 only 42 were left.

Station 7: The Carbon Cycle and the Keeling Curve

Carbon moves between the air, living things, the ocean, soil, and fossil fuels. Some processes add CO2 to the atmosphere; others remove it. Since 1958, scientists have measured the CO2 in the air at Mauna Loa, Hawaii. The graph of these measurements is called the Keeling Curve.

Shape based on the Mauna Loa record: about 315 ppm in 1958 and about 420 ppm in the early 2020s (ppm = parts per million).

Directions:

  1. Open Envelope 7. Sort the 10 process cards: adds CO2 to the air or removes CO2 from the air.
  2. Use your sort and the graph to answer the questions. Reset: put all cards back in Envelope 7.

Station 8: Quadrat Sampling and Biodiversity

Ecologists cannot count every plant in a big meadow. Instead they count a few random squares called quadrats and use them to estimate the whole population. They also compare biodiversity: how many different species there are and how evenly they are spread.

Directions:

  1. Use the Meadow page. Roll a die twice: the first roll is the row, the second is the column. That square is your quadrat.
  2. Count each species in that square. Do 4 quadrats in Meadow A and 4 in Meadow B. Record the counts.
  3. Find the average number of asters per square in Meadow A. Multiply by 36 (the number of squares) to estimate all the asters in Meadow A.
  4. Compare the two meadows and answer the questions.
Why roll a die? Random sampling keeps you from picking only the squares that look interesting.

Answer Key

Station 1: Abiotic or Biotic?

1. 1 sunlight (A); 2 pond water (A); 3 rocks (A); 4 oak tree (B); 5 deer (B); 6 mushrooms (B); 7 dead log (B); 8 cloud/air and wind (A); 9 soil (A; the living things in it are biotic); 10 hawk (B); 11 rabbit (B); 12 temperature (A).

2. Biotic: it was once living (part of a tree), and it is food and shelter for decomposers and insects.

3. Answers vary. Example: water lets the oak tree grow; sunlight lets grass do photosynthesis.

Station 2: Build a Food Web

1. Example: grass → grasshopper → mouse → snake (→ hawk).

2. Producers: grass, oak. Primary: grasshopper, mouse (seeds), rabbit, deer. Secondary/top: mouse (when eating grasshoppers), snake, fox, hawk, coyote. Decomposers: mushroom, soil bacteria.

3. Mice increase at first (fewer predators), which can mean less grass and seeds. Hawks lose one food source and eat more mice and rabbits; their numbers may drop a little. Changes ripple through the web.

Station 3: The 10% Rule

1. Primary: 2,000 kcal. Secondary: 200 kcal. Tertiary: 20 kcal.

2. 20 ÷ 20,000 = 0.1%.

3. It is used for life processes (movement, growth, body heat) and lost as heat from cellular respiration; some is in parts that are not eaten or not digested (waste).

4. So little energy reaches the top level that it can only support a small number of animals.

Station 4: Who Benefits?

1. 1: Mutualism (+ / +); 2: Parasitism (+ / -); 3: Commensalism (+ / 0); 4: Mutualism (+ / +); 5: Predation (+ / -); 6: Competition (- / -); 7: Mutualism (+ / +); 8: Commensalism (+ / 0).

2. A predator kills its prey (usually quickly) and eats it. A parasite lives on or in its host and harms it over time, usually without killing it right away.

Station 5: Population Growth Curves

1. An S-shaped curve that levels off near 270 thousand cells per mL.

2. Graph B (logistic). K is about 270 thousand per mL.

3. Running out of sugar (food), buildup of waste (alcohol), and limited space in the flask.

4. With no predators, the herd grew far above the carrying capacity and destroyed its slow-growing food supply (lichen). With no food left, most died: the population overshot and crashed.

Station 6: Predators and Prey: Lynx and Hare

1. About 10 years. 2. The hare peaks first. Lots of hares means lots of food for lynx, so the lynx population rises after the hares.

3. When hares become scarce, lynx cannot find enough food, so fewer lynx survive and reproduce.

4. Running out of food (plants), disease, or harsh winters. Accept any reasonable limiting factor.

Station 7: The Carbon Cycle and the Keeling Curve

1. Adds: Cellular respiration; Burning gasoline in a car; Decomposers breaking down dead leaves; Cutting down and burning a forest; A power plant burning coal; A volcano erupting. Removes: Photosynthesis; A forest growing back; CO₂ dissolving into the ocean; Ocean algae (phytoplankton) growing.

2. About 105 ppm (from about 315 to about 420). 3. Photosynthesis: in the Northern Hemisphere summer, plants take in more CO2, so levels dip; in winter, decomposition and respiration add it back.

4. Burning fossil fuels (cars, power plants) and cutting and burning forests.

Station 8: Quadrat Sampling and Biodiversity

1. Counts vary by quadrat. 2. Estimates vary; the actual number of asters in Meadow A is 71 (so an estimate near that is good).

3. Meadow A: it has all four species in similar numbers (totals 71, 80, 61, 75). Meadow B is mostly asters (totals 241, 22, 20, 5).

4. If one species dies, many other species can still fill its role (food, shelter), so the community keeps working. A meadow that depends on one species could collapse.
