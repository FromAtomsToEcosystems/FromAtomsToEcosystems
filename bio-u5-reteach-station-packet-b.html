Teacher Guide

There are 8 stations. Groups of 3 or 4 students rotate through them. Each station has a direction page and a few questions on the Station Recording Sheet. Students carry this one sheet from station to station. Most stations take about 15 to 20 minutes, so all 8 stations take about 2 to 3 class periods (in a 90-minute period, about 4 stations). You can also pick only the stations that match what your students missed on the exit tickets.

Some stations need no prep beyond printing the direction page. Others need cut-out cards in envelopes. Every station ends with a Reset step so the next group finds it ready.

Required materials

  • 4 envelopes: label them 1, 4, 5, 7
  • A cup with 10 beans of each of 5 colors (Station 8)
  • Scissors

Station 8: Bean Bottleneck

A bottleneck happens when a disaster leaves only a few survivors, chosen by chance. The survivors may not carry all the alleles the old population had. This is genetic drift.

Each bean is one individual. Each color is a different allele.

Directions:

  1. Check the cup: it should have 10 beans of each of 5 colors (50 beans). Count how many colors there are.
  2. Without looking, pull out 5 beans. These are the survivors of a flood. Record how many of each color.
  3. The survivors reproduce: for each survivor, count 10 beans of that color. That is the new population.
  4. Put everything back and repeat 2 more times. Then answer the questions.
  5. Reset: leave exactly 10 of each color in the cup.
You must not peek when you draw. The disaster is random!

Answer Key

Station 1: Evolution Word Match

1. Variation: Differences in inherited traits (Finches with deep and shallow beaks); Adaptation: An inherited trait that helps survival (A cactus's spines); Fitness: How many surviving offspring an organism leaves (A mouse with 9 pups that live); Natural selection: Better-suited individuals survive and reproduce more (Dark moths on sooty trees); Genetic drift: Allele frequencies change by chance (A storm kills random beetles); Gene flow: Alleles move between populations (A lion crosses a freeway and breeds); Speciation: A new species forms (Kaibab and Abert squirrels); Common ancestor: An earlier species that two groups came from (The ancestor of whales and hippos).

2. Genetic drift and gene flow.

Station 2: Superbugs: MRSA

1. About 58 percentage points (2% to 60%).

2. Some bacteria already had a resistance allele (variation). Antibiotics killed the others (selection). Resistant bacteria survived and reproduced, so resistance became more common.

3. No. The mutation was already there by chance; the antibiotic only selected for it.

Station 3: Natural Selection or Not?

1. 1: Not natural selection; 2: Natural selection; 3: Not natural selection; 4: Natural selection; 5: Not natural selection (as stated); 6: Natural selection.

2. Answers vary; must include existing variation, survival, reproduction, and inheritance.

Station 4: What Kind of Evidence?

1. Fossils: Tiktaalik, a fish with leg-like fins, about 375 million years old, Older rock layers hold simpler life forms; Anatomy: Whale flippers have the same bones as a human arm, Humans have a tailbone; Molecules: Human and chimp DNA are about 98 to 99% the same, Cytochrome c differs by 1 amino acid between humans and rhesus monkeys; Observed change: MRSA bacteria became more common in hospitals, Finch beaks got deeper after the 1977 drought.

2. All living things have DNA and proteins, so they can be compared even without similar body parts.

Station 5: The Peppered Moth Story

1. 3. No; the frequency of dark and light moths in the population changed.

Station 6: Whales With Legs?

1. They got smaller and smaller, until only tiny hip bones remain.

2. A vestigial structure.

3. They show transitional forms that change step by step from walking on land to living in the ocean.

Station 7: Selection, Drift, or Gene Flow?

1. Natural selection: Pesticide-resistant mosquitoes become common, Deep-beaked finches survive a drought; Genetic drift: A flood randomly kills half of a small beetle group, Only 20 elephant seals were left after hunting, so many alleles were lost, A few settlers start a new town; one rare allele becomes common there; Gene flow: Pollen blows from one field of corn to another, A mountain lion crosses a freeway and has cubs in a new group, Wolves from Canada join the wolves of Isle Royale.

2. In a small group, chance events can wipe out an allele or make it common quickly.

Station 8: Bean Bottleneck

1. Data varies; usually 1 or 2 colors are lost.

2. Usually not: only 5 random survivors, so some colors were not picked.

3. No. No color helped survival; it was chance (genetic drift).

4. Hunting left only about 20 to 100 seals, so many alleles were lost by chance; growing again did not bring them back.
