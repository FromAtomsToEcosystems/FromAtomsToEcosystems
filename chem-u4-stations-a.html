Unit 4 Stations A

Teacher guide: Unit 4 Stations A (the mole, dimensional analysis, molar mass, and mole map conversions)

Use after Day 5 (mole map practice). 8 stations, one per group of 4, about 10 minutes each. Students carry the paper reflection sheet or use chem-u4-stations-a-reflection.html on a Chromebook (it saves and sends scores to the Google Sheet).

  1. Print the station pages (one per table) and the card pages.
  2. Fold-over cards (Station 5): cut out each card, fold on the dashed line so the answer hides behind the question, and tape the fold.
  3. Optional hands-on add-ons: a balance with labeled cups holding 1 mole of water (18.0 g), salt (58.4 g), and sugar (342 g) at Station 1, and a periodic table at Stations 3, 4, 6, and 7.
  4. Rotation: 1 → 2 → … → 8 → 1, about 10 minutes each. Stations 4, 6, and 7 (calculations) take the longest. Put a periodic table at each calculation station.

How Big Is a Mole?

Chemists count tiny particles in groups, just like a "dozen" means 12 eggs. A mole is 6.02 × 10²³ particles. That number is called Avogadro's number.

It is hard to picture: 6.02 × 10²³ grains of sand would cover the whole United States several meters deep. A mole of pennies stacked up would reach past the edge of our galaxy. But atoms are so small that a mole of water molecules is only 18 grams, about one big spoonful.

Why such a strange number? It was chosen so that one mole of an element has a mass in grams equal to its atomic mass on the periodic table. Carbon's atomic mass is 12.01, so 1 mole of carbon atoms has a mass of 12.01 g. This mass of one mole is called the molar mass.
