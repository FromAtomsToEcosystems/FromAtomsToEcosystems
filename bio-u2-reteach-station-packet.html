Teacher Guide

There are 8 stations. Groups of 3 or 4 students rotate through them. Each station has a direction page and a few questions on the Station Recording Sheet. Students carry this one sheet from station to station. Most stations take about 15 to 20 minutes, so all 8 stations take about 2 to 3 class periods (in a 90-minute period, about 4 stations). You can also pick only the stations that match what your students missed on the exit tickets.

Some stations need no prep beyond printing the direction page. Others need cut-out cards in envelopes. Every station ends with a Reset step so the next group finds it ready.

Required materials

  • 7 envelopes: label them 1, 4, 5, 7 (and spares)
  • Scissors; card stock for Station 1 (optional)
  • Calculators (Station 3)
  • Color printing for Stations 2, 6, and 7 if possible
  • Optional real demos: naked eggs, leaf discs under a lamp, yeast flasks with balloons, onion slide with salt water

Station 1 setup: Build a Cell Membrane

Students build a phospholipid bilayer with paper pieces on a mat, add proteins, and decide which molecules can pass straight through.

  1. Print the Membrane Mat and the Station 1 cut-out page (card stock is best). Cut out all pieces and put them in Envelope 1.
  2. Set out the mat, the envelope, and the direction page.
Tip: If a group lines the phospholipids up in one layer, ask: "What is touching the water inside the cell?"

Station 2 setup: Which Way Will Water Move?

"Predict, then flip" cards: 8 real situations (blood cells, onion cells, a gummy bear, a slug, a goldfish). Students predict the type of solution and what happens, then check.

  1. Print the 4 Station 2 flip-card pages. Cut each page down the middle to make 8 tall cards.
  2. Fold each card on the dashed line so the answer hides behind the picture.
  3. Set out the cards and the direction page.
Tip: If you have a microscope, set up a slide of onion skin with a drop of salt water so students can see plasmolysis for real.

Station 3 setup: The Naked Egg Data

A data station: students calculate percent change in mass for shell-less eggs in three solutions and decide which were hypotonic or hypertonic.

  1. Set out the direction page and a few calculators.
  2. Optional: start real naked eggs (vinegar for 1 to 2 days, then the three jars) a few days before and set them out too.
Tip: Students often forget the negative sign for mass lost. Ask: "Did it get heavier or lighter?"

  1. Set out the direction page.

Tip: Cup D is the CO2 control. Students who say "it needs baking soda" should finish the sentence: baking soda provides carbon dioxide.

Station 7 setup: Photosynthesis vs. Respiration

A Venn diagram card sort comparing photosynthesis and cellular respiration.

  1. Print the Venn Diagram Mat and the Station 7 cards. Cut out the 12 cards and put them in Envelope 7.

Collect the recording sheets and check them with the answer key, or have groups trade and check. A quick follow-up: give the matching Day 2 exit ticket or warm-up from the unit to see who still needs help.

Station 1: Build a Cell Membrane

Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane. It is made mostly of phospholipids. Each phospholipid has a head that is attracted to water and two tails that are pushed away by water. Water is on both sides of the membrane, so the phospholipids form two layers: a bilayer. Proteins in the membrane act like doors. The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some things through and keeps others out.

Directions:

  1. Open Envelope 1. Use the phospholipid pieces to build a membrane in the band on the Membrane Mat. Think: where should the heads go? Where should the tails go?
  2. Add the channel proteins, the pump protein, cholesterol, and the carbohydrate chain where they belong.
  3. Use the 6 molecule cards. Decide which can slip straight between the phospholipids and which need a protein door.
  4. Answer the Station 1 questions. Reset: put every piece back in Envelope 1.

Station 2: Which Way Will Water Move?

Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a membrane. Water moves toward the side with more solute (more dissolved stuff, like salt or sugar).

Solution outside the cellCompared to the cellWater moves
Hypotonicless solute outsideinto the cell
Hypertonicmore solute outsideout of the cell
Isotonicsame solutein and out equally

Directions:

  1. Look at the 8 cards at this station. Do not open them yet.
  2. For each card, decide: is the solution hypotonic, hypertonic, or isotonic? What will happen to the cell? Write your prediction.
  3. When your team has predicted all 8, flip the cards open to check. Write the actual answer.
  4. Reset: fold every card closed.

Station 4: Passive or Active Transport?

Passive transport does not use the cell's energy. Molecules move from high concentration to low concentration (down the gradient), like rolling downhill. Diffusion, osmosis, and facilitated diffusion (through a protein) are passive.

Active transport uses energy from ATP. It moves molecules from low to high concentration (against the gradient) with protein pumps, or moves very large things in vesicles (endocytosis brings things in; exocytosis sends things out).

Directions:

  1. Open Envelope 4. Lay out the two heading cards.
  2. Sort the 10 example cards under Passive or Active.
  3. Record your sort and answer the questions. Reset: put all cards back in Envelope 4.

Station 5: Build the Photosynthesis Equation

In photosynthesis, plants, algae, and some bacteria use light energy to turn carbon dioxide and water into glucose (sugar). Oxygen is released. It happens in the chloroplast in two stages:

  • Light reactions happen in the thylakoids (the green stacks). Light energy splits water, releasing oxygen, and makes ATP.
  • The Calvin cycle happens in the stroma (the fluid around the stacks). It uses ATP and carbon dioxide to build glucose.

Directions:

  1. Open Envelope 5. Build the full photosynthesis equation with the cards. Two cards do not belong.
  2. Use the chloroplast picture to answer the questions about where each stage happens.
  3. Reset: put the cards back in Envelope 5.

Station 7: Photosynthesis vs. Respiration

Cellular respiration happens in the mitochondria of almost all eukaryotic cells, in plants and animals. It breaks down glucose with oxygen to release energy as ATP.

C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + ATP (energy)

Photosynthesis and respiration are like mirror images: the products of one are the reactants of the other.

Directions:

  1. Open Envelope 7. Place each card on the Venn Diagram Mat: photosynthesis only, respiration only, or both.
  2. Record your sort. Reset: put the cards back in Envelope 7.

Answer Key

Station 1: Build a Cell Membrane

1. The heads face the water (outside and inside). The tails hide in the middle, away from water.

2. O2 and CO2: straight through (small, nonpolar). Glucose: needs a protein (channel/carrier). Na+ and K+: need a protein (ions are charged); the pump moves them using ATP. (Water: a little slips through, but most goes through channel proteins called aquaporins.)

3. The membrane lets some molecules through but not others. Example: O2 passes straight through, but glucose and ions need protein doors.

Station 2: Which Way Will Water Move?

1. 1: hypotonic, swells/bursts. 2: hypertonic, shrinks. 3: isotonic, no change. 4: hypertonic, plasmolysis. 5: hypotonic, turgid (firm). 6: hypotonic, gummy swells. 7: hypertonic, slug loses water. 8: hypertonic, fish loses water.

2. The plant cell has a cell wall that pushes back and keeps it from bursting. Red blood cells have only a membrane, so they can burst.

Station 3: The Naked Egg Data

1. Jar 1: (41.0 - 62.0) ÷ 62.0 × 100 ≈ -34%, hypertonic. Jar 2: (75.8 - 61.5) ÷ 61.5 × 100 ≈ +23%, hypotonic. Jar 3: (56.4 - 63.0) ÷ 63.0 × 100 ≈ -10%, hypertonic.

2. The distilled water egg. The water had less solute than the egg, so water moved into the egg by osmosis.

3. It would gain mass (swell): the distilled water is hypotonic to the egg, so water would move back in.

Station 4: Passive or Active Transport?

1. Active: sodium-potassium pump; white blood cell swallowing a bacterium; releasing insulin in a vesicle; root cells pulling in minerals against the gradient. All others are passive.

2. Energy (ATP). 3. It moves from high to low concentration (down the gradient), so it needs no energy. The protein is only a door.

Station 5: Build the Photosynthesis Equation

1. 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6 O2

2. 6 N2 and "ATP only".

3. Light reactions: 1, thylakoid (grana stacks). Calvin cycle: 2, stroma. (3 is the outer membrane.)

4. From water (H2O is split in the light reactions).

Station 6: Floating Leaf Disc Data

1. Cup A rises to 10 by minute 15 (steep line); Cup B rises slower, to 6.

2. No light means no photosynthesis, so no oxygen is made and no discs float. Light is needed.

3. Carbon dioxide was missing (no baking soda). Without CO2, the Calvin cycle cannot make glucose, so photosynthesis stops and little oxygen is made.

Station 7: Photosynthesis vs. Respiration

1. Photosynthesis only: uses light energy, releases oxygen, uses carbon dioxide, makes glucose, happens in chloroplasts. Respiration only: uses oxygen, breaks down glucose, releases carbon dioxide, happens in mitochondria. Both: plants do it, makes ATP, happens in eukaryotic cells.

2. Plants do both. Plant cells have mitochondria and do cellular respiration all the time to make ATP.

Station 8: Yeast Balloons

1. Carbon dioxide. 2. More sugar made more gas: 0 g gave 3 cm, 20 g gave 27 cm. The increase slows down at high sugar (10 g to 20 g only added 5 cm).

3. The yeast mix may have had a little sugar or food already in it, or some air was in the balloon. Accept reasonable answers.

4. Aerobic respiration makes much more ATP (about 36 vs. 2 per glucose), so the cell gets far more energy from the same sugar.
